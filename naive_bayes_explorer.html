How Naive Bayes Works

Naive Bayes is a family of probabilistic algorithms used for classification. It relies on Bayes' Theorem to calculate the probability of a class given a set of features.

Bayes' Theorem

P(Class | Features) = [ P(Features | Class) × P(Class) ] / P(Features)

1 Prior: P(Class)

Our initial belief about how likely a class is, before looking at any evidence.

2 Likelihood: P(Features | Class)

If the item *is* in this class, what is the probability of seeing these exact features?

3 Evidence: P(Features)

The overall probability of seeing these features across all possible classes. (Often just used to normalize probabilities to 100%).

4 Posterior: P(Class | Features)

Our updated, final belief that the item belongs to the class after considering the evidence.

Why is it called "Naive"?

Real-world features often depend on each other. But Naive Bayes makes a "naive" assumption of conditional independence. It assumes that knowing the value of one feature gives no information about the value of another, once the class is known.

This simplifies the math drastically. Instead of calculating a complex joint probability, we just multiply the individual likelihoods together:

P(F1, F2, F3 | Class) = P(F1 | Class) × P(F2 | Class) × P(F3 | Class)

Key Insight: The Power of Absence

Notice that even when a word is unchecked, it influences the score! In Bernoulli Naive Bayes, the absence of a feature is treated as evidence. If "discount" is absent, we multiply by (1 - P(discount|Class)).

Key Insight: Exponents & Repeated Multiplication

If a word appears 3 times, we multiply its probability 3 times (i.e., probability raised to the power of 3). Unlike Bernoulli, absent words (count=0) are ignored in the likelihood calculation for Multinomial Naive Bayes.

Key Insight: Probability Density Function (PDF)

Unlike discrete features where we look up a distinct probability, for continuous data we plug the test feature (x) into the Gaussian PDF equation:
f(x) = (1 / (σ√(2π))) * e^(-0.5 * ((x - μ)/σ)^2) The height of the curve at the test point gives us the likelihood!
